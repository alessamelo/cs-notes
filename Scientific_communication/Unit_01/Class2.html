Communication - Concepts and Elements

Communication parts, processes, types, and related concepts

1. What Is Communication?

Communication is a process consisting of the exchange of messages between two or more people, living beings, or systems.

Communication is a transversal activity present throughout life processes. It can be understood as a voluntary act in which information, ideas, emotions, or intentions are exchanged.

Humans can communicate not only with other humans, but also with animals, machines, technological systems, and other environments.

Communication can also occur between cybernetic systems that imitate communication processes.

Example: A person speaking to another person, a person giving a command to a computer, or an animal responding to a sound are all examples of communication.

Communication generally occurs cyclically because the receiver can provide feedback and become the next sender. However, communication can also be unidirectional.

Communication usually seeks to produce a desired response in the receiver.

2. Importance of Communication

There is no known form of life that does not communicate in one way or another, either with its peers or with the external world.

Communication is therefore a fundamental process in the known world.

Technology has created mechanisms that amplify, characterize, and modulate communication, allowing information to travel across great spatial and temporal distances.

Key idea: Technology is transversal to communication because it allows humans to extend the range, speed, and capabilities of communication.

4. Source / Sender / Emisor

The source is the one who initiates the communicative act. It creates, encodes, and transmits a message.

The source must be capable of constructing a message and conveying it in a way that can be understood by the receiver.

Characteristics of an Effective Source

  • Credibility: The receiver must perceive the source as trustworthy. Personality, position, knowledge, and experience can influence credibility.
  • Attractiveness: The receiver's perception of the source can influence how effectively the message is received.
  • Persuasion: The source may use rational or emotional arguments to influence the receiver.

5. Message

The message is the information being transmitted. It is the object and purpose of the communicative process.

A message requires a system of signs and a code or language, such as Spanish, English, images, symbols, gestures, or sounds.

Messages can be transmitted orally, in writing, visually, or through other forms of communication.

Three Versions of a Message

  • Thought or projected message: What the sender intends to communicate.
  • Transmitted message: What is actually encoded and transmitted through the channel.
  • Received message: What the receiver decodes and understands.
Important: In effective communication, these three messages should be as similar as possible. In practice, they often differ because of ambiguity, misunderstanding, context, or noise.

6. Code

A code is a set of elements combined according to rules that allow them to have meaning.

The code allows the sender to transform an idea into a form that can be transmitted and understood by the receiver.

Example: When two people communicate in English, English functions as the shared linguistic code.

Codes do not necessarily have to be languages. They can include gestures, sounds, symbols, images, signs, or other systems of meaning.

Encoding and Decoding

Encoding is the process of converting an idea into a message using a code.

Decoding is the process of interpreting the received message using the code.

7. Channel

The channel is the physical or technological medium through which the message travels.

Examples: Sound waves carrying spoken words, ink on paper, electrical signals, radio waves, or digital networks.

Types of Channels

  • Personal channels: Allow direct communication and greater interaction between sender and receiver.
  • Impersonal channels: Can transmit messages to a large number of receivers, usually with little personalization.
  • One-way channels: Mainly transmit information from the source to the receiver without immediate interaction.
  • Bidirectional channels: Allow interaction between sender and receiver.
Key idea: The sender should select an appropriate channel according to the message, receiver, context, and communication objective.

8. Receiver

The receiver is the person, living being, or system that receives, decodes, and interprets the message.

The receiver performs a process inverse to that of the source:

  1. Receives the message.
  2. Decodes the signs.
  3. Interprets the information.
  4. May respond through feedback.

9. Noise

Noise is anything that interferes with the transmission, reception, decoding, or understanding of a message.

Noise can prevent communication from being completed effectively.

Examples: Physical noise, poor internet connection, unclear pronunciation, ambiguous words, distractions, cultural differences, or incorrect interpretation.

10. Context

Context is the extralinguistic environment in which the communicative act takes place.

Situational Context

Refers to the circumstances surrounding communication, such as political, historical, social, or physical conditions.

Thematic Context

Refers to the topic or subject being discussed, sometimes called the referent.

The same words can have different meanings depending on the context.

11. Feedback

Feedback is the response provided during the communication process.

Because of feedback, the sender and receiver can continuously exchange roles.

Example: Person A asks a question → Person B answers → Person A reacts to the answer → Person B responds again.

This creates a communication cycle rather than a simple one-directional transmission.

Communication can be compared to a ball game: one person sends something to another person, the other receives it and sends a response back.

Previous experiences influence this process. People interpret messages according to their knowledge, experiences, culture, emotions, and context.

Communication is therefore not always completely objective. The same message can be interpreted differently by different people.

13. Subjective and Objective Communication

Objective

Objective communication focuses on information that can be described or evaluated independently of personal interpretation.

Subjective

Subjective communication is influenced by personal experiences, emotions, beliefs, perceptions, and interpretations.

Example:

"The temperature is 30°C" is mainly objective.

"It is extremely hot today" is more subjective because the perception of heat differs between people.

14. Argument

An argument is an idea or claim supported by reasons or evidence that can be evaluated or demonstrated.

Arguments are commonly used when communication seeks to explain, justify, persuade, or defend an idea.

15. Assertive Communication

Assertive communication allows individuals to express thoughts, feelings, opinions, and needs clearly and respectfully.

Benefits

  • Facilitates healthy relationships by reducing misunderstandings.
  • Helps prevent and resolve conflicts.
  • Promotes mutual respect.
  • Helps people express their rights and needs appropriately.
  • Can improve self-confidence by allowing people to communicate clearly and respectfully.

16. Paralanguage

Paralanguage refers to vocal characteristics that accompany spoken language and contribute to meaning.

  • Voice
  • Tone
  • Rhythm
  • Volume
  • Intonation
  • Pauses
Paralanguage is complementary to the linguistic content of a conversation. The same words can communicate different meanings depending on tone, rhythm, or voice.

17. Verbal and Non-Verbal Communication

Verbal Communication

Verbal communication uses linguistic words and signs. It can be spoken or written.

Non-Verbal Communication

Non-verbal communication uses other types of signs, such as body movements, gestures, facial expressions, posture, and spatial distance.

18. Kinesics

Kinesics is the study of body movement as a form of communication.

The word is related to movement. It includes gestures, posture, facial expressions, and other body movements.

Palo Alto School

The Palo Alto School contributed significantly to the study of non-verbal communication.

Ray Birdwhistell studied body micro-movements and interactions, contributing to the development of kinesics.

The meaning of a movement is not universal. Culture can determine how particular movements and gestures are interpreted.

19. Proxemics

Proxemics is the study of how physical distance and personal space regulate social interactions.

Edward Hall

Edward Hall studied interpersonal distances and identified different spatial relationships, including:

  • Intimate distance
  • Personal distance
  • Social distance
  • Public distance

Erving Goffman

Erving Goffman's work examined how people present themselves and perform social roles during interactions.

20. The Human Body as a Communication Tool

The human body is an important tool for non-verbal communication. Through movement, dance, play, gestures, and posture, people communicate information and develop social abilities.

Communication Through Play

Games are especially important for learning and communication during the first years of life.

  • Provide different perspectives of reality.
  • Help establish relationships with the world.
  • Test skills and abilities.
  • Allow people to create and pursue goals.
  • Allow individuals to express aspects of their personality.
  • Encourage imagination, especially in children.
  • Allow repetition and renewal of roles or characters.

21. Communication and Previous Experience

Communication is influenced by previous experiences.

When a person receives a message, they do not necessarily interpret it independently of their previous knowledge. Their experiences, culture, expectations, emotions, and beliefs can influence interpretation.

Example: A word, gesture, or sound may have a different meaning for two people because they have different experiences or cultural backgrounds.

22. Communication and Technology

Humans have continuously developed technologies to extend communication.

Prehistoric humans used sounds, gestures, signs, and onomatopoeic expressions as forms of communication. Modern humans use complex linguistic, digital, audiovisual, and technological systems.

Technology allows communication to overcome spatial and temporal limitations.

Examples: Telephone calls, radio, television, email, social networks, videoconferences, messaging applications, and computer networks.

23. Audience and Communication Conditions

Effective communication requires considering the characteristics of the receiver or target audience.

The sender should consider:

  • Who is the audience?
  • What does the audience already know?
  • What language or code should be used?
  • What channel is appropriate?
  • What is the objective of the communication?
  • What are the communication conditions?
  • What possible sources of noise exist?
The sender should choose the most appropriate channel and code according to the message, receiver, context, and objective.

24. Interactive Speech Processing

Interactive speech processing involves the fundamental processing of spoken language and serves as a foundation for verbal communication.

It examines how people process speech and adapt their communication during interaction.

Mutual Understanding

  • Focuses on shared understanding between communication partners.
  • Participants create a common mental representation of the information being communicated.
  • People predict their partner's intentions, beliefs, and actions based on shared information.

Relationship Establishment and Maintenance

Verbal communication also has an important social dimension.

  • Emotional bonding
  • Trust
  • Cooperation
  • Relationship closeness

The quality of communication and the closeness between participants can influence the effectiveness of verbal communication.
