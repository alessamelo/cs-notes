Generative AI & Variational Autoencoders

Notes on generative models, latent spaces, Bayesian notation, and VAEs

← Back to NLP

1. Generative AI

Generative AI (GAI) refers to models that learn the underlying structure of data and can use that learned representation to generate new data.

Important correction: Generative AI does not normally create everything "without existing data." The model generally requires existing training data to learn patterns and distributions. After training, it can generate new samples that were not explicitly present in the training dataset.

For example, a generative model trained on images of faces can learn patterns involving shapes, textures, lighting, and facial features. It can then generate a new face that did not exist in the training dataset.

Traditional AI vs Generative AI

Traditional / Discriminative AI Generative AI
Often learns to predict a label or value from an input. Learns a data distribution and can generate new samples.
Example: classify an image as a cat or dog. Example: generate a new image of a cat.
Focuses on prediction or discrimination. Focuses on modeling and sampling from a distribution.

2. Autoencoders

Autoencoders are based on a simple but powerful concept: they take data, compress it into a lower-dimensional representation, and then reconstruct the original data from that representation.

Basic structure:

Input → Encoder → Latent Representation → Decoder → Reconstruction

Example

Suppose an image contains one million numerical values. Instead of representing the image using all one million values, the encoder may compress it into a latent vector containing only 10 to 100 numbers.

This compressed representation is called a latent representation. It exists in what we call the latent space.

x → Encoder → z → Decoder → x̂

Where:

  • x = original input data
  • z = latent representation
  • x̂ = reconstructed data

3. Why Not Just Use Autoencoders?

A standard autoencoder can learn useful latent representations, but its latent space is not necessarily organized in a way that makes it easy to generate new meaningful samples.

Problem: Irregular Latent Space

The encoder may place different examples in different regions of the latent space without guaranteeing that the space between those examples contains meaningful data.

Therefore, even if we take a point near an encoded image and use the decoder, the resulting point may produce an unrealistic or meaningless output.

Problem: Nearby points in the latent space do not necessarily correspond to meaningful variations of the original data.

Example

Imagine encoding an image of a digit "3". We might expect nearby latent vectors to decode into images of slightly different versions of "3".

However, with a conventional autoencoder, the latent space can be poorly structured. A nearby point could potentially decode into something completely different or produce an invalid output.

4. Variational Autoencoders

A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is a generative model that combines the idea of autoencoders with concepts from probability and Bayesian inference.

The VAE was introduced by Diederik P. Kingma and Max Welling in the paper Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes.

Diederik Kingma is also known for his work on the Adam optimization algorithm.

Why VAEs?

The main idea is to create a latent space that is continuous, structured, and suitable for sampling.

Instead of forcing each input image to map to one exact point, a VAE maps an input to a probability distribution in latent space.

5. Basic Bayesian Notation

Random Variable

A random variable is a variable whose value depends on the outcome of a probabilistic process.

For example, suppose a continuous random variable X can take values between 0 and 10.

Probability Density Function

The probability density function (PDF) describes how probability is distributed over possible values of a continuous random variable.

p(x)

Expectation

The expectation represents the average or expected value of a random variable.

E[X]

Marginal Distribution

A marginal distribution describes the probability distribution of one variable while ignoring another variable.

If we have a joint distribution over X and Z, we can obtain the marginal distribution of X by integrating out Z.

p(x) = ∫ p(x,z) dz

Conditional Probability

Conditional probability describes the probability of one variable given another variable.

p(x | z)

Likelihood

The likelihood describes how probable the observed data is under a particular model or parameter configuration.

p(x | z)

In the VAE context, this conditional distribution is commonly interpreted as the probability of generating an observation x given a latent variable z.

6. General Idea of a VAE

Our goal is to generate new data from an unknown data distribution p(x).

For example, suppose our dataset contains images. The distribution p(x) represents the underlying distribution of possible images.

The problem: We do not know the exact shape or properties of the true distribution p(x). We only have access to samples from it, such as the images contained in our training dataset.

Working directly with this high-dimensional distribution can be difficult. Therefore, VAEs introduce a latent variable z.

Latent Distribution

The latent variable is a lower-dimensional representation of the information contained in the observed data.

z ∈ Latent Space

Instead of modeling the complicated data distribution directly, we define a simpler prior distribution over the latent variables.

A common choice is a standard normal distribution:

p(z) = N(0, I)

7. The Two Important Distributions

The VAE uses two important conditional distributions to connect the observed data space and the latent space.

1. Posterior / Encoder

The encoder approximates the posterior distribution of the latent variable given an observed data point.

qφ(z | x)

This asks:

"Given this image x, what latent vectors z are likely to represent it?"

The encoder does not normally output one deterministic latent vector. Instead, it outputs the parameters of a probability distribution, usually a mean and variance.

μ(x), σ²(x)

2. Likelihood / Decoder

The decoder models the likelihood of generating an observation given a latent vector.

pθ(x | z)

This asks:

"Given this latent vector z, what data x can be generated?"

8. Sampling in a VAE

The key difference between a traditional autoencoder and a VAE is that the encoder represents each input as a probability distribution rather than a single fixed point.

For a given input x, the encoder produces:

qφ(z | x) = N(μ(x), σ²(x))

We then sample a latent vector from this distribution:

z ~ qφ(z | x)

The sampled latent vector is passed to the decoder:

z → Decoder → x̂

Because the latent representation is probabilistic, different samples can produce slightly different reconstructions.

9. Reparameterization Trick

There is a problem with directly sampling from a distribution: stochastic sampling makes ordinary backpropagation difficult.

VAEs solve this using the reparameterization trick.

Instead of directly sampling:

z ~ N(μ, σ²)

we sample random noise:

ε ~ N(0, I)

and construct z using:

z = μ + σ ⊙ ε

where ⊙ represents element-wise multiplication.

This separates the random component ε from the learnable parameters μ and σ, allowing gradients to flow through the sampling operation.

10. Why the Latent Space Becomes Useful

A VAE does not only learn to reconstruct data. It also regularizes the latent distributions so that they remain close to a chosen prior distribution.

Usually:

p(z) = N(0, I)

This encourages the latent space to become continuous and organized.

Consequently, nearby points in the latent space are more likely to correspond to meaningful variations of the generated data.

For example, moving gradually through latent space may produce gradual changes in an image rather than completely unrelated outputs.

11. VAE Loss Function

The VAE objective contains two main components:

  1. Reconstruction loss — encourages the decoder to reconstruct the input accurately.
  2. KL divergence — encourages the learned latent distribution to remain close to the prior distribution.
Loss = Reconstruction Loss + KL Divergence

Reconstruction Term

The reconstruction component measures how different the generated output is from the original input.

Reconstruction Loss ≈ -Eq(z|x)[log p(x|z)]

KL Divergence

The KL divergence measures the difference between the encoder's latent distribution and the chosen prior distribution.

DKL( qφ(z|x) || p(z) )

Therefore, the VAE objective can be written as:

L = -Eqφ(z|x) [log pθ(x|z)] + DKL ( qφ(z|x) || p(z) )

12. How a VAE Generates New Data

Once the VAE has been trained, we do not need an existing image to generate a new sample.

Instead, we sample a latent vector from the prior distribution:

z ~ N(0, I)

Then we pass that latent vector through the decoder:

z → Decoder → x̂

The decoder generates a new sample according to the learned distribution.

Generation process:

Sample z from the prior
↓
Pass z through the decoder
↓
Generate new x

14. Key Ideas to Remember

  • Generative AI learns from existing data but can generate new samples after training.
  • Autoencoders compress data into a lower-dimensional latent representation and reconstruct it.
  • A conventional autoencoder does not necessarily produce a well-structured latent space.
  • A VAE represents an input as a probability distribution rather than a single deterministic point.
  • The encoder approximates qφ(z|x).
  • The decoder models pθ(x|z).
  • The latent space is commonly regularized toward N(0,I).
  • The reparameterization trick allows stochastic sampling while maintaining differentiability for backpropagation.
  • The VAE objective balances reconstruction quality and latent-space regularization.
  • After training, new data can be generated by sampling z ~ N(0,I) and passing it through the decoder.

15. Conceptual Summary

The central idea of a VAE is to learn a structured latent space from which we can generate new data.

Data x → Encoder → q(z|x) → Sample z → Decoder → Generated x

During training, the encoder learns how to represent data in latent space while the decoder learns how to reconstruct data from latent vectors.

The KL-divergence term prevents the latent space from becoming arbitrarily irregular by encouraging the learned distributions to remain close to a known prior.

This makes it possible to sample points from the latent space and decode them into new, meaningful data.
